# 摄影点评评分 Rubric V5.16（原版协调边界候选）

Use this rubric for every `摄影点评` critique. The goal is learning diagnosis for beginner photography, not contest judging. Scores must be discriminative; avoid clustering most images at 6-7.

## Core Scale

Use 5/10 as the baseline for an ordinary record photo: the image is recognizable and viewable, but has weak photographic intention, weak subject control, or obvious beginner problems. Being clear, readable, and informationally useful is not enough to move a photo above record level.

The scale must have working low and high ends:

- Serious basic failures must go below 5. Do not protect a photo at 5 just because the subject is identifiable.
- A severe basic failure usually belongs in the 3-4.5 range.
- A normal basic failure usually caps the photo at 5. A mostly ordinary documentation photo usually belongs around 4.5-5.5, and may approach 6 only when it has a clear photographic strength beyond record value.
- If a basic failure exists but the image has unusually strong expression, light, composition, or moment, it may reach 5-5.5, or approach 6 only when the compensating strength is clearly visible.
- Good beginner photos can and should reach 7.5, 8, or 8.5 when evidence supports it. Do not hold a clearly strong image at 7.5 only because minor fixable issues can be found.

| Score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1-2 | Failed image. The subject is unreadable, exposure/focus/motion problems make the image nearly unusable, or there is no meaningful visual information. |
| 3-3.5 | Severe basic failure. There may be a subject, but focus, shake, exposure, direction, or subject readability is badly broken. |
| 4-4.5 | Clear hard flaw. The photo is understandable, but a basic failure blocks normal viewing or makes the image feel technically/structurally failed. |
| 5 | Ordinary record photo or basic-problem photo. It can be understood, but the image is mostly documentation, or a basic flaw caps it. |
| 5.5-6 | Useful beginner attempt or stronger-than-average record photo. There must be at least one visible photographic strength, such as cleaner composition, useful light, mood, subject relationship, or a controlled moment; clarity alone is not enough. |
| 6.5-7 | Shareable beginner photo. Subject is clear, basic technique holds, background and edges are mostly controlled, and either composition or light shows deliberate intent. Still has fixable weaknesses. |
| 7.5 | Strong beginner photo with at least two clearly good dimensions, but one visible limitation still meaningfully holds it back. |
| 8-8.5 | Clearly strong photo. At least two of composition, light, timing/story, and technical control are clearly strong; weaknesses are secondary and do not break viewing. Use 8.5 only when the image has a memorable visual idea, moment, mood, or formal control beyond ordinary strong beginner work. |
| 9 | Portfolio-level photo. Strong intent, strong execution, and no obvious beginner flaw. Rare. |
| 10 | Reference-level photo. Exceptional and nearly flawless. Almost never use for normal learning critique. |

## Dimension Meanings

### Technical

Evaluate exposure, focus, motion blur, sharpness, noise, white balance, and basic image quality.

- 1-2: Unusable due to severe blur, extreme exposure failure, or subject unreadability.
- 3-4: Major technical flaw dominates, such as missed focus on the subject, strong hand shake, blown highlights on the subject, or muddy underexposure.
- 5: Technically readable but visibly flawed, or simply competent enough for record use.
- 6: Mostly acceptable, with one meaningful flaw; the technical handling must be cleaner than a basic readable record.
- 7: Stable exposure and clarity, minor issues only.
- 8: Clean, controlled, and technically confident.
- 9-10: Excellent technical control; reserve for rare cases.

### Composition

Evaluate subject clarity, simplification, edge control, horizon/verticals, crop, visual balance, layers, visual path and landing point, and intentional use of center/symmetry/negative space.

- 1-2: No readable subject or chaotic framing.
- 3-4: Subject exists but is badly blocked, too small, cut awkwardly, or surrounded by dominant distractions.
- 5: Record-level framing; subject is present but not visually controlled.
- 6: Clear subject with some visual organization, but crop, edge control, visual balance, or landing point is visibly weak. A merely centered/readable subject or line without clear destination stays around 5.
- 7: Deliberate framing with acceptable edge/background control, stable visual weight, and a clear visual path or landing point; the image should feel intentionally organized, not just legible.
- 8: Strong visual organization, depth, and eye guidance.
- 9-10: Highly refined composition; every major element feels intentional.

### Lighting

Evaluate quality, direction, contrast, dynamic range, color temperature, atmosphere, and how light supports the subject.

- 1-2: Light destroys the image: subject is hidden, key details are clipped, or mixed light is incoherent.
- 3-4: Harsh, flat, or misdirected light strongly weakens the photo.
- 5: Usable light, but ordinary, uncontrolled, or only sufficient for documentation.
- 6: Some useful light or mood, with clear limitations; the light must add more than basic visibility.
- 7: Light supports the subject and gives shape or atmosphere.
- 8: Light is a major strength.
- 9-10: Exceptional light; rare.

### Story

Evaluate whether the photo gives the viewer a reason to look from visible image evidence: moment, relationship, gesture, scale, mood, contrast, place, or visual idea. Separate three levels of evidence: record information, scene/mood, and relationship/moment/visual idea. Practical record value, product information, damage evidence, readable signage, object usefulness, event explanation, or location identification is not the same as photographic story.

No people does not mean no story: light, scale, space, negative space, weather, color, or formal relationships can create visual narrative. People or objects also do not automatically create story: there must be visible gesture, expression, relationship, mood, conflict, contrast, or a clear visual idea.

- 1-2: No subject or no meaningful viewing reason.
- 3-4: Subject is present but the image says very little.
- 5: Basic record value only: the image identifies an object, place, product, damage, sign, or event, but has no clear visible mood, relationship, moment, or visual idea.
- 6: A readable mood, place, scale relationship, atmosphere, or simple subject relationship exists beyond simple information value.
- 7: Clear visual idea, gesture, relationship, contrast, spatial scale, or moment.
- 8: Strong mood, relationship, timing, narrative tension, or memorable visual idea from the image itself.
- 9-10: Memorable story or visual idea; rare.

### Color Observation

Color is not a sixth score. Evaluate it explicitly in every critique as a visible experience that can affect lighting, composition, story, technical quality, and editing advice.

Check:

- dominant color impression: warm, cool, neutral, high-saturation, low-saturation, monochrome, mixed, or muddy;
- harmony: whether the main colors support each other, clash productively, or compete without order;
- color hierarchy: whether color helps the viewer find the subject, creates separation, or creates distraction;
- emotional effect: whether color supports mood, season, time of day, place, intimacy, distance, tension, calmness, or visual style;
- technical/editing issue: white balance, color cast, oversaturation, dull grayness, clipped color channels, skin tone, dirty shadows, or inconsistent local edits;
- post-processing direction: whether the image should be warmed, cooled, desaturated, selectively saturated, simplified, contrast-adjusted, or color-balanced.

Do not reward color only because it is vivid. Strong color must support subject, mood, structure, or story. Do not punish unusual color when it is intentional and visually coherent. If color is ordinary but harmless, say so briefly and focus advice elsewhere.

### Overall

Overall is not a simple average. It measures whether the photo works as a photograph.

Prioritize:

1. Is the subject clear?
2. Does the image have a reason to exist beyond record value?
3. Does any hard flaw block viewing?
4. Are light and composition helping the subject?
5. Is the photo useful evidence of beginner progress beyond simple documentation?

## Hard-Flaw Caps

Apply these caps strictly for beginner photos. A hard flaw is a basic issue that blocks normal viewing or prevents the subject from working. Apply this narrow exception only to “Main subject is present but unclear or weak”: if that row would otherwise trigger solely because there is no isolated subject or local landing point, do not apply it when the visible subject is the field as a whole and is immediately legible either as comparable repeated units covering most of the frame, or as two to three simple large regions organized by a shared boundary, with a traceable rhythm, direction, grouping, boundary, or balance. Similar objects or large regions alone are insufficient, and the exception is unavailable when another cap or a competing fragment, shadow, gap, crop, or weight defeats that order. It changes nothing if the row would not otherwise trigger, removes only that row, and creates no strength or points. Its qualifying facts establish only that normal viewing is not blocked; intentional effect, dimension strength, story beyond record value, and high-score eligibility each require separate visible evidence.

### Orientation Handling

Before applying any direction, rotation, or tilted-frame cap, actively check orientation sources. For archived local images, first read `metadata.json`, then inspect the local `original.*` with a metadata-capable source; do not rely on one viewer or one metadata command when it may miss EXIF orientation. If any available source reports EXIF orientation, a trusted visible-orientation note, or a trusted gallery/user display orientation, judge the photograph after that orientation is applied; never use the raw pixel matrix alone to mark a rotation error. When a raw preview appears sideways but orientation metadata or trusted display information exists, do not apply a rotation cap unless an explicitly orientation-corrected rendering is also checked and still blocks normal viewing. If a raw-pixel view appears sideways but the EXIF-corrected image or gallery/user display is upright, do not count this as a photographic orientation error.

Orientation handling is an internal scoring check. In the critique text, do not mention metadata filenames, preview filenames, tools, commands, specific EXIF orientation values, camera metadata, or file metadata. Describe only the visible result when it affects scoring, such as whether the displayed image appears upright or whether rotation visibly blocks normal viewing.

Only treat rotation as a hard flaw when the correctly oriented display still has clear evidence that normal viewing is blocked: people, buildings, waterlines, text, furniture, or other gravity-dependent subjects remain sideways/upside down, and rotating the image would make the scene logic clearly more natural. Mild tilt, perspective distortion, unusual framing, or intentional abstract rotation are not rotation-error caps; evaluate them under composition or intentional-effect rules instead.

| Condition | Required cap |
| --- | --- |
| Main subject is unreadable | Overall max 4.0; Composition max 4.0; Story max 4.0 |
| Main subject is present but unclear or weak | Overall max 5.0; Composition max 5.0; Story max 5.0 |
| Main subject is severely out of focus by accident | Technical max 3.5; Overall max 4.0 |
| Intended-subject detail or landing edge is visibly softer than nearby secondary detail, or no stable sharp anchor remains and defining texture is erased; fewer than two intentional-effect tests pass | Technical max 4.5; Overall max 5.0 |
| Severe hand shake or motion blur makes the intended subject hard to read | Technical max 4.0; Overall max 4.5 |
| Obvious hand shake or motion blur weakens the intended subject | Technical max 5.0; Overall max 5.5 |
| A broad important-subject highlight erases internal tone, color separation, or texture needed for the subject to work; recognizable outline alone does not cancel the cap | Technical max 4.5; Lighting max 4.5; Overall max 5.0 |
| A broad important-subject shadow erases internal tone, color separation, or texture needed for the subject to work; recognizable outline alone does not cancel the cap | Technical max 4.5; Lighting max 4.5; Overall max 5.0 |
| Horizon/verticals are unintentionally tilted and visible | Composition max 6.0; Overall max 6.5 |
| At normal viewing size, a distraction, crop, edge cut, or imbalance repeatedly pulls attention away after the subject is found, covers or truncates a defining part, blocks the intended path or structural boundary, or leaves a conspicuous partial fragment or unbalanced mass with no compositional role; do not trigger when an out-of-frame form clearly strengthens path, rhythm, framing, or balance | Composition max 5.5; Overall max 6.0 |

| Photo is mostly ordinary documentation, evidence, product/signage record, damage/usefulness record, or event explanation with no visible photographic story | Story max 5.0; Overall max 5.5 |
| Light is flat/ordinary and story is weak | Lighting max 5.5; Story max 5.5; Overall max 5.5 |
| Multiple major flaws from different dimensions appear together | Overall max 4.5 |

## Basic-Flaw Escape Rule

If a basic flaw exists, start from the relevant cap. Only raise the overall score into 5-5.5 when the photo has at least one genuinely strong compensating quality, and only approach 6 when that strength is clearly visible:

- strong light or atmosphere;
- strong composition despite the flaw;
- strong moment, expression, gesture, or story;
- a clear intentional effect that works;
- rare or visually interesting subject matter handled with some control.

Do not raise a basic-flaw or ordinary-documentation photo above 6 unless the flaw is minor and the image has two or more clear photographic strengths.

## High-Score Eligibility

Apply every hard-flaw cap before this section. Nothing here removes or offsets a real cap.

Before giving 7.5 or higher, confirm that the subject or intended subject relationship is immediately legible, no severe technical hard flaw applies, background, edges, crop and visual weight are controlled, and the image has visual intention beyond record value.

For 7.5, require visible 7-level evidence in at least two dimensions. “Clearly good” means matching that dimension’s 7-level description, not being exceptional or free of minor flaws. One visible device may support two dimensions only when it produces two separately observable results, such as organizing the visual path while also creating mood, scale, relationship, contrast, or a moment. Do not require a third strong dimension.

A clear landing point may be one subject, a linked subject system, or a controlled sequence, repetition, reflection, or spatial relationship. It qualifies only when the viewer can immediately tell what belongs together and no competing distraction, crop, edge cut, or unbalanced weight diverts the main reading.

Use 7.5 when at least two dimensions have clear 7-level evidence but one visible limitation still meaningfully weakens the core effect. Use 8 when at least two dimensions have clear 8-level evidence and remaining flaws are secondary. Use 8.5 only when those conditions are met and the image also has a memorable visual idea, moment, mood, or formal control.

A weak third dimension or minor fixable flaw does not by itself cancel high-score eligibility. It blocks the high tier only when it weakens subject readability, the main visual path or relationship, intended mood, or core technical result at normal viewing size. If it reaches a hard-flaw condition, apply that cap first. If stopping at 7.5, name the specific limitation that prevents 8.

If a cap applies, mention it briefly in the critique. Example: `由于主体明显虚焦，技术分和总体分被封顶。`

## Intentional Effects vs Accidental Flaws

Do not punish intentional blur, intentional darkness, high-key brightness, intentional high contrast, unusual framing, small subject placement, negative space, backlit silhouette, emotional direct flash, rough documentary texture, or collage/sequence structure as a hard flaw if the image provides enough evidence that the choice is deliberate and effective.

Treat an effect as intentional only when at least two of these are true:

- The blur or darkness supports a clear subject, mood, motion, or visual idea.
- There is another sharp or visually stable anchor in the frame.
- The composition appears controlled: edges, subject placement, and visual path still work.
- The effect is selective rather than accidental-looking across the whole frame.
- The image contains a recognizable genre convention, such as panning, long exposure water, light trails, silhouette, abstract motion, deliberate shallow depth of field, high-key minimalism, low-key mood, direct-flash snapshot, or collage/sequence storytelling.
- The user's prompt explicitly says the effect was intentional, and the image result supports that claim.

If fewer than two are true, treat it as an accidental beginner flaw.

Examples:

- Good intentional motion blur: a moving subject streaks through a stable background, and the blur creates direction or energy.
- Accidental motion blur: the whole frame is smeared, with no stable anchor and no clear reason.
- Good intentional shallow focus: the chosen subject detail is sharp while the background falls away.
- Accidental missed focus: background or random foreground is sharper than the intended subject.
- Good low-key image: important subject contour remains readable and darkness creates mood.
- Accidental underexposure: important subject detail is lost and the darkness does not add meaning.
- Good negative space or small-subject image: the small subject has a clear relationship to the surrounding space, scale, mood, or motion.
- Accidental small subject: the subject is merely lost, with no clear visual relationship or landing point.
- Good direct-flash or rough snapshot: the flash, closeness, gesture, or sequence strengthens the mood, intimacy, or event energy.
- Accidental harsh flash: the flash flattens the subject or creates glare without adding mood, relationship, or story.

When uncertain, say so and score conservatively: do not give high scores just because a flaw might have been intentional.

## Scoring Discipline

- Use the full 1-10 range when evidence supports it.
- Score each image independently from visual evidence and this rubric; do not apply post-hoc distribution correction, score remapping, forced curves, or batch normalization.
- For beginner work, 5 is not an insult; it means the photo is understandable but mostly record-level.
- Do not let serious basic failures sit at 5 by default; use 3-4.5 when focus, shake, exposure, direction, or subject readability is badly broken.
- Do not use 6.5 as the default safe score, and do not give 6+ merely because the subject is clear, exposure is usable, lines are present, or the photo has practical record value.
- Give 7.5, 8, or 8.5 when the image has clear strengths in at least two dimensions and no severe hard flaw; use minor limitations to choose within that range, not to automatically cap strong images at 7.5.
- Give 9 only for portfolio-level images.
- Do not give 10 unless the image is exceptional.
- Scores may use one decimal place when the image evidence supports that level of distinction. This applies to technical, composition, lighting, story, and overall scores.
- Avoid fake precision: use one decimal only to express a concrete judgment, not to make the score look scientific. Do not use more than one decimal place.
